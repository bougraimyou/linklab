While I was writing the tests for a telecom link budget, I found a bug in my own code that would have understated every optical link by more than 100 dB.

I had put free space path loss into the fibre calculation. It is a radio formula. Light inside a glass core does not spread out the way a radio wave does, so the term has no business being there at all. The tool looked completely plausible, and it would have confidently told you that a perfectly good 42 km span could not work.

So I took it out, moved free space path loss into the microwave engine where it actually belongs, and wrote a test that fails if the term ever comes back.

That grew into LinkLab, and it now does three things.

For fibre it works out attenuation, splices, connectors and the chromatic dispersion limit, then tells you the maximum reach your optics can carry.

For point to point microwave it does free space path loss, EIRP, both antenna gains, feeder losses, and the rain fade you need to leave room for.

For copper it applies the TIA limits, including the insertion loss derating that kicks in when you use more than two pairs.

It also checks the design before it documents it. A port can only take one cable. A panel has to physically fit in its rack. A cable has to land on a port that actually exists. When something is wrong it tells you all of it at once rather than one error at a time.

Then it generates the documentation. The cable schedule, the link budgets with the working shown rather than just a verdict, and a rack elevation for every rack with each cable drawn out to its far end.

The numbers come from published ITU and TIA values, and every formula in the test suite is checked against a hand computed figure instead of against whatever the code happened to produce. Fifty three tests, no runtime dependencies, and the documentation site is rebuilt and published on every push.

I built it because my degree is in telecommunication systems engineering, and I kept running into the fact that the calculators and spreadsheets I needed for the kind of work I wanted were either subtly wrong or gave me a verdict with nothing behind it. I wanted a tool that showed me which term closed the link.

It runs locally and it is public.

If you have used it, I would genuinely like to hear what you think. Did it give you a verdict you trusted, and did anything look wrong or feel missing? Even "the rain fade allowance should default to something other than zero" is useful to me.

I am also looking for people who work on fibre or microwave links day to day and would be willing to tell me where the model is too simple.
